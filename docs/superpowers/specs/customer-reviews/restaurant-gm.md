# Customer review: restaurant-gm (Andre)

**Who I am:** Andre, 41. GM and part-owner of an independent 90-seat restaurant and bar in Dallas with a private room. Toast is my POS, OpenTable runs reservations, and Instagram plus the phone fill my nights. I have been sold "all-in-one" before. I'm not convinced a "CRM" belongs in a restaurant at all.

**How I looked:** Phone first (390x844), then desktop (1440x900), on all four versions: home, CRM, websites, industries, the restaurant pages, plus the "See your business" pop-up on the current site and the Ember & Oak sample website. Screenshots are in `scratchpad\customer-reviews\restaurant-gm\`.

**One thing that applies to every version:** not one page mentions Toast or OpenTable. That's the first question I'd ask, and nobody answers it. If I take on your reservation page and your AI receptionist, do I drop OpenTable? Do the two fight over the same tables? Does my guest history in Toast come over? Until I know, I can't picture this in my building, however nice the page looks.

---

## Current site (updated)

**1. First 10 seconds on the phone.** The homepage says "Run the whole business from one app" and "From $99 a month, no setup fee, no contract" right next to the button. So I get the price. What I don't get is that it's for me: the first screen is "Good morning, Travis" on a sample HVAC company. The restaurant page is different. "Every Empty Seat Is Gone Forever" is a bit dramatic, but the paragraph under it names a ringing phone and a held table sitting empty at 8. That one made me keep scrolling.

**2. Built for a business like mine?** This is the only version that argues the case for a restaurant at all. The Friday 6:45 story, plating while the phone rings, the no-show four-top, the rainy Tuesday when I can't reach my regulars: that's my week. Then it falls apart in places. Five "gaps" and not one is about private events, and the private room is where my real margin is. The pop-up does a good job, though: pick Restaurants & Bars and you see the Ember & Oak website and its CRM stacked together. That's the clearest way anyone has shown me "your site and your system match."

**3. Trust builders and killers.**
- Builds trust: pricing is plain everywhere, the "Who owns my customer list?" answer on the homepage is a flat "You do," and there's a real local number.
- Kills it: all three testimonials come from a garage door company, a contractor and a car lot. None from a restaurant.
- Kills it: the "Hire it yourself: $4,520+/mo" table. I don't pay a $3,390 receptionist. My host answers the phone. The comparison also says "$99/mo, one bill" when the phone line is another $50, so the line I'm supposed to trust most is the one that leaves something out.
- Kills it: the stats cite ringfoods.com, popmenu.com and olo.com. Those companies sell restaurant software, so it reads like a sales deck.
- On the phone, the restaurant page is about 21,000 pixels long. The chat bubble and the "See your business" pill sit on top of the content and the bottom Call bar the whole way down.

**4. Unanswered questions.** Toast and OpenTable. Private-room deposits (the events board screen shows them, but the copy never mentions them). Who updates the menu on the $499 site when it changes every week, and what that costs. How many logins I'd need: hosts, managers and bartenders put me over 10. What the AI receptionist sounds like.

**5. Would I tap "Try the CRM"?** Maybe. The restaurant page sold me on the problem. The fine print says I fill out a form and someone calls me, which is not "try." I'd rather call (469) 833-3033 and ask about Toast first.

**6. Favorite / least favorite.** Favorite: the Friday-rush gap section. Someone thought about a dining room for that one. Least favorite: garage-door testimonials on a restaurant page, next to a cost table that tells me I'm paying a receptionist I don't have.

## Concept A

**1. First 10 seconds on the phone.** Clean and clear. Headline, two buttons, then four check marks: $99 for up to 10 users, no setup fee, month to month, websites $499. Fastest read of the four. It also looks like every software company's site, and the first screen I see is a home services dashboard.

**2. Built for a business like mine?** The restaurant CRM page leads with "tonight's book and private events, handled." That's the right first line. The "What owners get" box (covers at a glance, floor plan for the host stand, events board from inquiry to final bill) is the clearest summary of what I'd actually use. But the page has no restaurant argument. It's a list of screens. On the phone those screens are desktop screenshots shrunk to 390 wide, and I tapped one and nothing happened. The events board, the one screen that would win me over, is unreadable on a phone.

**3. Trust builders and killers.** Builds trust: the "How it works" 1-2-3 (tap, we call you, send your first invoice) and the "What you won't run into" list (no contract, no setup fee, same price every month). Kills it: bright blue generic SaaS look, no testimonials at all, FAQ answers hidden behind taps, and "send your first invoice" as the finish line. I don't invoice diners, I run checks through Toast.

**4. Unanswered questions.** Same Toast/OpenTable hole. The same FAQ dodges as C (see below), only folded up so you don't notice them.

**5. Would I tap "Try the CRM"?** No. Nothing on this version shows it understands restaurants beyond the sample screens.

**6. Favorite / least favorite.** Favorite: the "What owners get" box on the restaurant page. Least favorite: you can't read the screens on a phone, and tapping them doesn't enlarge them.

## Concept B

**1. First 10 seconds on the phone.** Dark purple, big type, nav tabs right on the phone screen (Home, CRM, Websites, Industries), which I liked. Price bullets show up just under the buttons. Clear enough, but it looks like an app for crypto or gaming, not hospitality.

**2. Built for a business like mine?** Same restaurant copy as A, but the phone screens are laid out best here. Server "My tables," the guest reservation page and the AI call transcript run nearly full width, and on a phone I could actually read the allergy band and the "table for 6 at 7" call. Stacking the CRM steps as 01 to 05 on the homepage is easy to follow, even if the steps are about techs and estimates.

**3. Trust builders and killers.** Builds trust: the restaurant page puts "Signature plan, $99 a month, 1 to 10 users" right next to the screens, and the closing section says "No contract. No setup fee." Kills it: animation and scroll effects make it feel slick and salesy, no proof from real owners, and the desktop owner screens are still thumbnails.

**4. Unanswered questions.** Toast/OpenTable, logins over 10, private-room deposits, menu updates on the site.

**5. Would I tap "Try the CRM"?** Maybe. Being able to read the server and reservation screens on my phone moved me a little.

**6. Favorite / least favorite.** Favorite: phone screens big enough to read. Least favorite: it looks like a tech startup, not someone who has stood at a host stand.

## Concept C

**1. First 10 seconds on the phone.** Cream paper and a serif headline, and a red "Try the CRM" button. The top bar says "Based in DFW, Texas" with the phone number before anything else. The price bullets come just below the fold. I knew what it was and what it cost in about two thumb flicks. It feels like a person made it, more like a good menu than a software ad.

**2. Built for a business like mine?** "A day at Ember & Oak" is the best framing of the four. It treats my business as a place with a day, not a "vertical." The Industries page lists every industry's day on one long page, so I can see restaurants weren't bolted on. Same weak spot as A, though: the restaurant screens on the phone are desktop thumbnails, and there's no "why a restaurant needs this" argument at all. The sample website preview at the bottom looks right for a chophouse.

**3. Trust builders and killers.**
- Builds trust: every FAQ answer is open on the page, so I don't have to tap through. "Month to month, and you can stop whenever you want" and "What's the true total cost?" (the plan, plus $50 a line, plus usage above normal, website optional) are the most honest lines on any version.
- Kills it: having everything open also shows me how often they duck. Nine answers say some version of "we go over that on your demo call": setup time, who sets it up, bringing my list over, who owns my data, card rate, payout timing, staff permissions, Spanish, support hours. "Who owns my data?" answered with "we'll discuss it" is a red flag. The current site just says "You do."
- No testimonials anywhere.

**4. Unanswered questions.** Toast/OpenTable, card rate, payout timing, data ownership, staff permissions (my bartenders should not see the events P&L), Spanish (half my kitchen and some of my front of house), support hours on a Saturday night.

**5. Would I tap "Try the CRM"?** Maybe, leaning yes. It's the version I trust most, and the events board is exactly the tool I'd want for the private room. I'd still call first to ask about Toast.

**6. Favorite / least favorite.** Favorite: "A day at Ember & Oak" plus FAQ answers I can read without tapping. Least favorite: the nine "on your demo call" answers. It reads like a car lot that won't put the price on the windshield.

## My ranking

1. **Concept C.** Most trustworthy, most human, looks closest to hospitality, and puts everything on the table, even when what's on the table is a dodge.
2. **Current site.** Weaker brand trust (the wrong testimonials, the inflated cost table), but the restaurant page is the only one that argues why a restaurant needs this, and the "See your business" pop-up is the best side-by-side.
3. **Concept B.** Best phone screens, too slick and too tech-startup for me.
4. **Concept A.** Clear but generic, and the restaurant page is a gallery of screens I can't read.

## 8 changes I'd make to the winning direction (Concept C)

1. **Answer Toast and OpenTable on the restaurant page.** Add a block near the top titled "Works next to Toast and OpenTable?" and say it straight: what this replaces, what it sits beside, whether reservations sync or double up, and whether guest history comes over. This is the first question every restaurant owner will ask, and without an answer I'm out.

2. **Lead the restaurant page with the private room, and make the events board readable on a phone.** Put the Events & large parties screen first, full width, tap to enlarge, with one line under it: "Inquiry, proposal, deposit, final count, balance." *(Steal from B: phone screens big enough to read.)* That screen, with deposits in hand, the final-count reminder and "Text Jenna for the final count," is the one thing that would make me believe a CRM fits a restaurant. Right now it's a thumbnail in third place.

3. **Replace every "we go over that on your demo call" with a real answer.** At minimum: data ownership ("You do. Your guest list, number, website and domain stay in your name." *Steal from current site*), the card rate, payout timing, a setup time range ("most restaurants are live in X days"), who sets it up, and Saturday-night support hours. One "let's talk about your case" is fine. Nine reads as hiding.

4. **Add the restaurant argument, short.** *(Steal from current site, cut down hard.)* Three tight beats instead of five long ones: the Friday rush call, the no-show four-top, and the private-room inquiry that sat in Instagram DMs for two days. Keep the stories and drop the vendor-sourced stats. I trust "6:45 on a Friday" more than a percentage from a company selling me something.

5. **Show a restaurant cost example, not a crew of 6.** "A 90-seat restaurant with 14 staff logins and one phone line: $199 + $50 = $249 a month." Also say what "normal use" on a phone line means in calls or minutes, because a restaurant phone rings a lot more than a plumber's.

6. **Make "Try the CRM" honest, and show who calls me.** Either let me click around Ember & Oak's CRM myself, or rename the button "Get a walkthrough." Then add the 1-2-3 under it *(steal from A: "How it works")* with a name and face for who calls, when ("within one business day"), and "no card needed."

7. **Restaurant proof, or say plainly you're new to it.** Put no garage-door quotes on a restaurant page. One quote from a bar or restaurant owner, or one honest line ("Restaurants are newer for us; here's what we'll set up and how we'll check in the first month"), beats three quotes from the wrong industry.

8. **Answer the website questions a restaurant actually has.** On the restaurant website page: who updates the menu when it changes weekly, and is that included. Can a private-event inquiry form drop straight onto the events board. Does it pull in my Instagram. Hosting and domain costs after the $499. Also fix the sample site, which says "Rated 4.9 on Google" in the hero and "4.8 average guest rating" further down. A buyer notices a sample that disagrees with itself.

**Other things I'd steal:** the current site's "See your business" pop-up idea, as a "pick your industry" strip near the top of C's homepage so a restaurant visitor never lands on an HVAC dashboard first. And A's "What you won't run into" list, which C already mostly has.

## Would I tap "Try the CRM"?

On Concept C, maybe, leaning yes, but only after a phone call that answers the Toast/OpenTable question. If change #1 and change #2 were on the page, it would be a yes.
