The call your front desk misses is the new patient who books somewhere else

A new patient calling for the first time almost never leaves a voicemail. If the line is busy or rolls over, they scroll down and call the next practice, and the one after that, until a real voice picks up and gets them scheduled. Your front desk is not ignoring them. They are checking a patient out, on hold with an insurer verifying benefits, or turning a room over, and the phone rings through to voicemail while they are a few feet away with their hands full. The call that got missed was not a wrong number. It was a new patient, and a new patient is worth years of cleanings, fillings, the occasional crown, and the friends and family they refer once they trust you.

A dental answering service answers on the first ring, no matter what the front desk is in the middle of. It greets the caller warmly, answers the questions that come up on almost every new-patient call, and gets them onto the schedule while they are still on the line. The new patient is booked instead of lost to the practice down the road that happened to pick up.

Built around the calls a dental front desk actually fields

Not every call is an emergency, but the phone at a dental office almost never stops. People call to ask whether you take their insurance, to move an appointment, to start as a new patient, or because a filling fell out at dinner and they want to be seen tomorrow. Every one of those is a real person deciding whether your practice is easy to deal with, and a voicemail box cannot answer a single question or book a single visit.

  • Answers days, evenings, and weekends, so the new mover searching on a Sunday and the patient with a broken tooth on a holiday reach a real answer instead of a recording.
  • Handles the routine questions that tie up the front desk: your hours, where you are, whether you take their insurance, and what a first visit looks like.
  • Collects new-patient details and books the appointment straight onto your calendar, so your team is not re-keying it or playing phone tag later.
  • Tells an existing patient with a lost crown or real pain apart from a general question, and flags the urgent ones to your team the moment they come in.

It handles patient details the way a dental office has to

A dental practice cannot treat phone conversations casually, because the moment a caller starts describing what is wrong they are sharing health information. The answering service is set up with that in mind. It collects only what it needs to book the visit, keeps that information inside your systems instead of scattering it, and is built to handle patient details with the care a medical or dental office is held to. Your team decides what it asks, what it stores, and what it repeats back, so a first-time caller gets the same discretion on the phone that they would get in your chair. Nobody is left describing their symptoms to a voicemail that anyone might play back later.

You own the number, the patients, and the schedule it fills

This runs on your existing office number, or a new one registered to your practice, not to us. Every caller, every new-patient record, and every appointment it books is yours, exportable any time, so the patient list you are building stays an asset you own instead of something you rent back month to month. There is no long contract holding your data hostage. The answering service is one piece of the Top Shelf platform, so a call it answers lands in the same CRM that sends the reminders and recall notices, and the new patient it books today is the one your system keeps bringing back for years.

How it plays out

A Saturday toothache, on the schedule before Monday

It is Saturday afternoon and a man three towns over cracks a tooth on a popcorn kernel. He searches for a dentist, calls the first two he finds, and both ring to a weekend voicemail. Yours answers, asks a few calm questions, sees he is in real pain, and books him first thing Monday while flagging it to your team as urgent. He stops calling around because someone finally helped him. You open the office Monday to a new patient already on the schedule, with his name, his insurance, and the problem noted, instead of hearing he found a dentist who picked up.

Yes, those are the calls it handles best. It can tell people your hours, where you are, whether you accept their plan, and what a first visit involves, and it books or reschedules right on your calendar. You decide exactly what it is allowed to say about coverage.

Yes. It is built to treat what a caller shares with the discretion a dental office is held to, collecting only what it needs to book the visit and keeping it inside your systems. You control what it asks and what it stores.
